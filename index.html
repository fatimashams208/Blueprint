    Learn More: Water Quality & Treatment

    This section explains the water-quality parameters used by the analyzer, why they matter, and the treatment methods that appear in its recommendations. Use it as a project reference while entering values and interpreting the results.

    1. Water-quality parameters

    Turbidity

    Unit: NTU (nephelometric turbidity units)

    Turbidity describes how cloudy water appears because suspended particles scatter light. It can be caused by silt, clay, organic matter and other fine particles.

    Why it matters: High turbidity can interfere with disinfection and may indicate that clarification or filtration is needed.

    Hardness

    Unit: mg/L as CaCO₃

    Hardness is mainly associated with dissolved calcium and magnesium ions. It is commonly reported as an equivalent concentration of calcium carbonate.

    Why it matters: Hard water can form scale in pipes, heaters and boilers. Some applications require lower hardness, while others may need minerals to remain present.

    Total Dissolved Solids (TDS)

    Unit: mg/L

    TDS is an estimate of the total dissolved material in water, including dissolved salts and minerals.

    Why it matters: High TDS can affect taste, scaling, corrosion and suitability for sensitive industrial or biological uses. TDS alone does not identify which substances are present.

    pH

    Unit: pH units (dimensionless)

    pH indicates how acidic or alkaline water is. It is related to hydrogen-ion activity; in a simplified dilute-solution calculation, pH = −log₁₀[H⁺].

    Why it matters: pH affects corrosion, scaling, chemical reactions and aquatic organisms. The current main input form does not measure initial pH; pH is used in custom target limits.

    Temperature

    Unit: °C

    Temperature affects reaction rates, gas solubility, biological activity and the performance of some treatment processes.

    Why it matters: Different applications operate at different temperatures. The current main input form does not ask for initial temperature; it is available as a custom target range.

    Chlorine

    Input in this project: Present / Absent

    Chlorine is often used as a disinfectant to control microorganisms in water. Residual chlorine may be unwanted in some processes or sensitive applications.

    Why it matters: The analyzer uses a qualitative selection, not a measured chlorine concentration in mg/L.

    Heavy metals

    Input in this project: Traces / Present

    Metals such as lead, arsenic, cadmium and mercury can be harmful at sufficiently elevated concentrations. Their behaviour depends on chemical form and water conditions.

    Why it matters: A laboratory test is needed to identify and quantify individual metals. The project's selection is only a simplified flag.

    Chemical pollutants

    Input in this project: Present / Absent

    This broad category can include organic compounds, industrial chemicals, pesticides and other contaminants.

    Why it matters: Different chemicals require different treatment methods. A single present/absent value cannot identify the pollutant or guarantee its removal.

    Biological contaminants

    Input in this project: Present / Absent

    This category refers to microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses and protozoa that may create health risks.

    Why it matters: Disinfection can reduce microbial risk, but its effectiveness depends on the organism, water clarity, dose and operating conditions.

    Dissolved oxygen (DO) & biochemical oxygen demand (BOD)

    Units in laboratory reporting: mg/L

    DO is the oxygen dissolved in water. BOD estimates the oxygen used by microorganisms while breaking down biodegradable material under a defined test.

    Why it matters: DO and BOD help describe aquatic and wastewater conditions. In this project they are represented by four qualitative categories, not measured values.

    2. Treatment methods used by the recommendation engine

    The following explanations cover the methods named in the current script. The analyzer selects methods using simplified rules based on input values and the selected target profile.

    Coagulation and flocculation

    Coagulation involves adding a suitable coagulant so that fine suspended particles lose some of their stability. Flocculation gently mixes the water to help the destabilized particles join into larger flocs that can settle or be filtered.

    These steps are commonly used when fine particles are too small to settle quickly on their own. Chemical selection and dose normally require jar testing and process control.

    Sedimentation

    Water is held or passed through a basin at a controlled flow so that heavier suspended particles or formed flocs can settle under gravity. Settling reduces the solids load on later filters.

    Sand and multimedia filtration

    Water passes through a porous bed that captures suspended particles. A multimedia filter uses layers of media with different properties to distribute particle capture through the bed. Filters need suitable operation and periodic cleaning or backwashing.

    Microfiltration and ultrafiltration

    These membrane processes use small pores to separate suspended particles and many microorganisms from water. Ultrafiltration generally has finer separation than microfiltration. Neither process should be assumed to remove dissolved salts reliably.

    Ion-exchange softening

    A cation-exchange resin exchanges calcium and magnesium ions for other ions, commonly sodium. This reduces hardness and helps control scale formation. The resin eventually needs regeneration or replacement.

    Lime-soda precipitation

    Lime and soda ash are used under controlled chemical conditions to convert some dissolved calcium and magnesium into less-soluble compounds. The resulting precipitates are separated by clarification and filtration.

    Nanofiltration and reverse osmosis (RO)

    Pressure pushes water through a selective membrane. Nanofiltration can reduce hardness and some dissolved substances; reverse osmosis generally removes a broader range of dissolved salts. Both processes produce a concentrated reject stream and require suitable pretreatment and operating controls.

    Deionization and electrodeionization

    Deionization uses ion-exchange materials to remove dissolved ions. Electrodeionization combines ion-exchange media and an electric field to continuously move ions through selective membranes. These methods are often used for high-purity water after membrane pretreatment.

    Activated carbon and chemical dechlorination

    Activated carbon can adsorb many organic compounds and can remove free chlorine under suitable conditions. Sodium bisulfite is a reducing agent that can chemically neutralize chlorine. Choice and dose depend on the actual water chemistry and process requirements.

    Specialized media for metals and chemical pollutants

    Activated alumina, iron-oxide-based media and other adsorbents can capture selected contaminants under suitable conditions. A solid carbon block with specialized media may reduce certain organic chemicals. No single media removes every metal or pollutant; the contaminant must first be identified.

    Ultraviolet (UV) disinfection

    UV light can inactivate many microorganisms by damaging their genetic material. UV does not remove dissolved salts or particles, and it does not leave a disinfectant residual in the water. Water clarity and correct equipment operation are important.

    Biological treatment and aeration

    Biological processes such as activated sludge and trickling filters use microorganisms to break down biodegradable pollutants. Aeration transfers oxygen into water and can support biological treatment or increase dissolved oxygen. Actual performance depends on loading, temperature, residence time and system design.

    4. How this project makes recommendations

    1. The user enters selected initial water-quality values and qualitative contaminant flags.
    2. The user chooses an application or enters custom target ranges.
    3. The script compares selected inputs with the target profile and applies its programmed decision rules.
    4. It displays the treatment methods associated with the conditions detected by those rules.

    The parameter calculators are separate utilities. Their results are not automatically transferred into the main treatment form.
